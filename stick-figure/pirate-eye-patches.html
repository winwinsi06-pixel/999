Why did pirates wear eye patches?

Short answer: mostly because they'd lost an eye. The famous theory is that a patch kept one eye ready for the dark below deck. It works in tests, but no record shows pirates actually did it.

1The obvious answer

Sea battles were brutal. Plenty of sailors really had lost an eye. But there's a cleverer theory that gets passed around a lot.

2The problem: the dark hold

Walk from blazing sun into a dark ship's hold and you're nearly blind. Your eyes need 20 to 30 minutes to fully adjust to the dark. In a fight below deck, you don't have 30 minutes.

3The trick: switch eyes

Wear the patch up on deck, so one eye stays in the dark all day. When you drop into the hold, move the patch to the other eye. The eye that was covered is already used to the dark, so you can see straight away.

Open eye: the one that was out in the sun. He can't see a thing.

4Does it actually work?

Yes. In 2007 the TV show MythBusters ran people through a dark obstacle course. Switching to the eye that had been under a patch made a big difference, and they rated the idea plausible.

The biology is real: the rod cells you use in dim light take a long time to recover after bright light. It's why submarine crews and astronomers use dim red light at night.

5The catch

Nobody has found a pirate logbook, diary or trial record that mentions the trick. Meanwhile, ships were full of things that took eyes out: wood splinters from cannon fire, sword cuts, exploding gunpowder.

Verdict

Most pirate eye patches probably covered a real injury. The switch-eyes trick works and makes sense, but it's a modern theory with no historical proof that pirates used it.
